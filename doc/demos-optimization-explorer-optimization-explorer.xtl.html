programdemos/optimization-explorer/optimization-explorer.xtl

Optimization explorer, part 1: is downhill one direction? One fixed loss, L(x, y) = (x^2 + 3 y^2) / 2, its contours and its gradient field (a field that comes from a potential); gradient descent on it under different metrics, the same start and the same step size policy: the plain step (the metric is the identity), a step under the metric diag(a, 1) with a as the control, and Newton's direction (the loss's own curvature as the metric). The objective never changes; the path and the number of steps do. The steepest direction at one point under two metrics, worked in full: the unit ball of each and the direction that lowers the loss most within it. Then the same question on a curved space: the unit vector that makes x' A x largest (the leading eigenvector) found by a step that stays on the sphere (Manifold's) against a flat step repaired afterward.

ᵘe̲ta : (Float, Float) -> Float

function · line 80

A positive-definite matrix G is the metric: the length of a step v is sqrt(v' G v). The update is p <- p - eta P g, P the inverse of G (a step under a metric: X_eTaL-ML's by GM4, written out). The step size policy, the same for every metric: eta is the exact line search along the direction d = P g, the one number that makes the loss least along that line, which for this loss is (g . d) / (d . H d); so the metric chooses the direction and nothing else, and a metric and twice the metric give the same path. A direction of zero length (the floor reached) stays put.

ᵘe̲ta ← { (g, d) → den ← '+ r̲/ d × H ᵐˣm̲ul d◆ den = 0.0 ? 0.0◆ ('+ r̲/ g × d) ÷ den }
Used in: ᵘs̲tep, ᵘf̲irst

ᵘs̲teepest : Num a => a -> Float

function · line 135

At the start, the direction that lowers the loss most among those of length 1 under the metric G: v = -P g / sqrt(g . P g), the one point of G's unit ball where the loss falls fastest. Under the identity the ball is the unit circle and v is -g / |g|; under H it is the ellipse x^2 + 3 y^2 = 1 and v points more along x. The rate of fall along each, g . v, is a slope per unit of that metric's length, so the two rates are in different units and are not compared. Checked by sampling: of 360 directions round each ball, the one with the smallest g . v agrees with the formula to the sampling's half a degree.

ᵘs̲teepest ← { G → P ← ᵐˣi̲nverse G◆ d ← P ᵐˣm̲ul g0◆ (0.0 − d) ÷ s̲qrt '+ r̲/ g0 × d }

The unit vector p that makes the quotient f(p) = p' A p largest, for a small symmetric A, is A's leading eigenvector, which Matrix finds (Jacobi's sweeps) for the check. The gradient of f in the room is 2 A p; the constraint is the unit sphere. Two ways up from the same start, the same rate, the same number of steps: (a) a step that stays on the sphere, Manifold's s_phereDescend against -2 A p (the gradient's part along the sphere, then the exponential map); (b) a flat step p + rate 2 A p, which leaves the sphere, repaired by scaling back to length 1. And (a) with the cheap retraction in place of the exact step. For each step: the quotient, how far the point is from the eigenvector along the sphere (either sign, since -p is the same line), and how far off the sphere the step went before any repair.
