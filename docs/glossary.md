# Glossary

One name for one thing (plan, principle 4). Every term the pages use,
in plain words for a reader who may know neither geometry nor machine
learning: what it is, a small example, what it is not confused with
here, and the first demo that uses it. The rows are written in the
columns of the pages' glossary (X_eTaL-ML's `shared/lesson`
`glossary.tsv`, read at ask GM1: an id, the term, the explanation, a
worked example, the demo that teaches it), so step shell-adoption can
turn them into `shared/glossary.tsv` unchanged; the "not" clause is
part of the explanation, as a reader of the page needs it there too.

The words a newcomer meets first are at the top, in the order the
manifold-tour page introduces them; the rest follow by track.

## The space

| id | term | explanation | example | first demo |
| -- | ---- | ----------- | ------- | ---------- |
| manifold | manifold | A space that is flat up close: look at a small enough piece of it and it is, as near as you like, an ordinary flat space of some dimension. The earth is round and the floor is flat, and both are true: the floor is a small piece of the earth. A circle is a manifold of one dimension, the skin of a ball one of two. Not every curved set is a manifold: a surface with a corner or an edge (a cube's skin, a polytope) is not flat at the corner, and here is not called one. | On the sphere, two points a tenth of a radian apart are 0.1000 apart along it and 0.0999 straight through: nearly the same, as on a floor. Two points a quarter turn apart are 1.571 along it and 1.414 through: not the same. | manifold-tour |
| intrinsic-dimension | intrinsic dimension | How many directions you can move in along a space: the number of coordinates its own map needs. An ant on the skin of a ball can go north or east, two directions, so the skin has intrinsic dimension two, whatever room it sits in. Not the number of coordinates of the room (that is the ambient dimension). | The circle: one (its map is one angle). The sphere, the torus and the Swiss roll: two. | manifold-tour |
| ambient-dimension | ambient dimension | The number of coordinates of the room a space is drawn in: how many numbers each point is written with. The skin of a ball drawn in a room of three coordinates has ambient dimension three and intrinsic dimension two; the difference is what makes it curved inside the room. Not the intrinsic dimension; the two agree only when the space is the room itself (the Poincare disk is drawn in its own two). | A word embedding of 64 numbers has ambient dimension 64; if the words lie near a curved sheet of few directions, its intrinsic dimension is small. | manifold-tour |
| locally-flat | locally flat | What makes a manifold a manifold, said as a measurement: between two points close together, the straight-line distance through the room and the shortest distance along the space nearly agree. Far apart, they do not. | Swiss roll, points a and b a little apart round the sheet: 1.626 through the air, 1.633 along the sheet. Points a and c one turn apart: 6.28 through the air, 52.8 along the sheet. | manifold-tour |
| geodesic | geodesic | The shortest path between two points measured along a space, as against the straight line through the room around it: what the string pulled tight on the surface does. On a sphere it is an arc of a great circle, the route an airplane flies; on the Poincare disk, an arc of a circle meeting the rim at right angles, or a diameter. Not a straight line, except on a flat space. | From p to r on the sphere, the geodesic is 0.873 long and the straight line (the chord through the inside) 0.518. | manifold-tour |
| manifold-hypothesis | the manifold assumption | The assumption that the points a model learns from, though written with many numbers, lie near a curved thing of few directions inside that room: a data manifold. It is an assumption made so that a method can be built on it, not a fact about every data set, and these pages call it one (the literature's "manifold hypothesis"). Not a statement that a learned representation is a single smooth manifold; it need not be. | The Swiss roll: three numbers per point, two directions along the sheet. | manifold-tour |
| data-manifold | data manifold | The curved thing a data set lies near, under the manifold assumption: the points themselves, in the room they are given in. Not a representation manifold, which is a space a model is made to put its points on by choice; the two are kept apart here, because an experiment can match one to the other or not. | The Swiss roll is a data manifold; the sphere a classifier puts its unit-length representations on is a representation manifold. | manifold-tour |
| polytope | polytope, and the Birkhoff polytope | A shape with flat faces, straight edges and corners, like a cube or a diamond, in any number of dimensions: the set of points that satisfy some linear inequalities. The Birkhoff polytope is the set of doubly stochastic matrices of one size; its corners are the permutation matrices (Birkhoff, von Neumann). A polytope is flat in pieces but not locally flat at a corner or an edge, so it is not a smooth manifold and is not called one here: a method that stays inside one is "constrained to a polytope", not "on a manifold". | The 3 by 3 doubly stochastic matrices: nine entries, four free, a polytope of dimension four with six corners (the six permutations). | mhc-explorer |
| tangent-vector | tangent vector and tangent space | At a point of a manifold, a direction you can move in without leaving it, with a length: an arrow lying flat against the surface there. All such arrows at one point make its tangent space, a flat space of the manifold's intrinsic dimension: the floor the ant stands on. A vector of the room is made tangent by dropping its part that points off the surface. Not a point of the manifold: an arrow at a point. | On the unit sphere at p = (1, 0, 0), the vector (2, 3, 0) has tangent part (0, 3, 0): its part along p is dropped. | sphere-hello |
| metric | metric | The rule that gives the length of a tangent vector, and so the distance between nearby points, at every point of a space: the ruler. Change the rule and the same points are different distances apart, the same step a different size. Not the distance function itself (which the metric determines) and not a norm on the whole room: it may differ from point to point, as the Poincare disk's ruler shrinks toward the rim. | On the Poincare disk, a step of 0.035 on paper near the middle is 0.070 by the disk's ruler; the same step near the rim is 3.87. | manifold-tour |
| curvature | curvature (zero, positive, negative) | How a space departs from flat, measured by what happens to triangles and circles: on a flat space (curvature zero) a triangle's angles add to a half turn and a circle's circumference is 2 pi times its radius; on a sphere (positive) the angles add to more and circles are shorter; on a hyperbolic space (negative) the angles add to less and circles grow faster than their radius, exponentially so. A number per point, which can vary; here mostly one number for the whole space (the sphere's is 1, the Poincare disk's is -1, a plane's 0). Not the same as dimension or topology: a sphere and a torus both have intrinsic dimension two and different curvature and topology. | The Poincare disk's ruler: the marks 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 from the middle sit at 0.46, 0.76, 0.91, 0.96, 0.99 on paper. | representation-explorer |
| topology | topology | What is left of a shape when lengths and angles are forgotten and only connectedness remains: how many pieces it has, how many holes, whether a loop on it can be shrunk to a point. A sphere and a cube's skin have the same topology; a sphere and a torus do not (the torus has a hole). Not geometry: two spaces of the same topology can have different curvature and metric. A flow that can be run backward cannot change topology: it cannot tear one piece into two. | A circle and a square are the same topologically; a circle and a line are not (cut the circle once and it is still one piece; the line is already two ends). | manifold-flows |
| exponential-map | exponential map and retraction | Taking a step: from a point, along a tangent vector, staying on the manifold, for a distance equal to the vector's length. The exponential map is the exact step, along the geodesic (on the sphere: cos t p + sin t v / t). A retraction is any cheaper step that agrees with it for small vectors (on the sphere: add the vector and scale back to length 1). Its inverse, the arrow from one point to another, is the logarithmic map. Not an exponential of a number: the name is historical. | From p = (1, 0, 0) along (0, pi/2, 0): a quarter turn, landing on (0, 1, 0). | sphere-hello |
| frechet-mean | mean on a manifold | The point of the manifold whose summed squared distances along it to a set of points is least (the Frechet or Karcher mean): the average that stays on the space. The plain average of points on a sphere falls inside the sphere; the mean on the sphere is found by stepping repeatedly along the average of the arrows to the points, which cancel at the mean. Not the plain average put back on the space, which is a different point. | Three points on the sphere (the axes): the plain mean is 0.42 inside; the mean on the sphere is their common direction. | sphere-hello |
| distortion | distortion | How badly a set of distances is kept when points are put into a space: for every pair, the ratio (or the difference) of the distance the space gives them to the distance they should have, summed or averaged over the pairs. Zero when every distance is kept. A tree's distances cannot be kept in a flat space of few dimensions (the number of nodes grows exponentially with depth); in a hyperbolic space they nearly can. Not a model's accuracy on a task: a representation can be accurate and distorted, or faithful and useless, and the two are measured apart. | A tree of depth 4 embedded in the plane, on the sphere and in the Poincare disk at the same dimension: three distortion numbers, compared. | representation-explorer |

## Fields and flows

| id | term | explanation | example | first demo |
| -- | ---- | ----------- | ------- | ---------- |
| vector-field | vector field | A direction and a speed at every point of a space: an arrow drawn at each point, saying where a thing there would go next. Wind on a map is one. A state that follows the field traces a path, a trajectory. Not a single vector, and not a gradient field unless it comes from a potential. | On the plane, the field (-y, x) turns every point round the origin: a rotation. | dynamics-explorer |
| gradient-field | gradient field and potential | A vector field that comes from a single function (the potential; in training, the loss): the field is that function's gradient, the arrows pointing the way it rises fastest, or minus its gradient, the arrows pointing the way it falls fastest (minus the gradient of L is the gradient of -L, so both are gradient fields). Gradient descent follows minus the loss's gradient. Such a field cannot circle: going round a loop and coming back, the function is where it was. A general vector field need not come from any function; a pure rotation does not. Not every vector field is a gradient field, and that is why gradient descent and a flow are different things. | The field (x, 3 y) is the gradient of (x^2 + 3 y^2) / 2, and (-x, -3 y), the field gradient descent follows on it, is minus that gradient; the rotation (-y, x) is the gradient of nothing. | dynamics-explorer |
| divergence-curl | divergence and curl | Two numbers that summarize a vector field at a point: the divergence says whether the field spreads out (positive) or draws in (negative) there; the curl says whether it turns, and which way. A gradient field has no curl; a rotation has no divergence. Not properties of a single arrow: they are about how the arrows change from point to point. | The rotation (-y, x): divergence 0, curl 2 everywhere. The field (x, y): divergence 2, curl 0. | dynamics-explorer |
| flow-integrator | flow and integrator | A flow is the motion of every point under a vector field over time: where each starting point ends after a while. An integrator is the arithmetic that follows a field step by step from a start (Euler: one step along the arrow; Runge-Kutta: a weighted average of several looks ahead), with an error that depends on the step size and the method. Not the field itself: one field, many integrators, and they do not land in the same place. | Following the rotation with Euler steps of 0.1 for one turn spirals outward; a fourth-order Runge-Kutta stays near the circle. | dynamics-explorer |
| rest-point | rest point and its stability | A point where a vector field's arrow is zero, so a state placed exactly there stays (a fixed point). It is stable if states set down near it fall in, and unstable if some move away. For a field v(x) = A x the rest point is the origin, and the eigenvalues of the matrix A decide: both real parts negative, every path falls in; one positive, some path leaves; both zero (a pure rotation), paths circle at their distance. A saddle has one of each sign: paths come in along one line and leave along another. Not a minimum of a loss: a rotation's rest point is the minimum of nothing. | The attractor (-1 0 / 0 -0.5): eigenvalues -1 and -0.5, every path falls in. The saddle (1 0 / 0 -1): 1 and -1. | dynamics-explorer |
| loop-sum | the sum round a loop | Walk round a closed loop and add up, piece by piece, the field's arrow dotted with the piece of the walk (how much the walk went with the field): the field's circulation round the loop. For a gradient field it is zero, because each piece adds the fall of the loss along it and the loop ends where it began; so a nonzero sum shows a field comes from no loss. Green's theorem says it equals the curl added up over the area inside. Not a length or an area: it can be negative, and zero for a loop that is not small. | The rotation (-y, x) round the square from (-1, -1) to (1, 1): each side adds 2, the sum is 8, the curl 2 times the area 4. | dynamics-explorer |
| two-player-game | two-player game and its solution | Two learners sharing one objective, one making it small and the other large, each moving only its own numbers along its own slope: the setting of a GAN (a generator against a discriminator) at its smallest. The solution (an equilibrium) is a point where neither can do better by moving alone. The two slopes together make a vector field that need not come from any loss, so the players can circle the solution instead of settling into it. Not a single loss minimized by both: one player's loss is the other's gain. | f(x, y) = x y: player one lowers it with x, player two raises it with y; the solution is (0, 0) and the players' field is the rotation (-y, x). | dynamics-explorer |
| extragradient | look-ahead step (extragradient) and taking turns | Two repairs for players who circle. The look-ahead step (Korpelevich's extragradient, 1976): each player steps to a trial point, looks at the slopes there, and then steps from where they started along those slopes; on the plain game it brings the players in, at two looks at the slopes per step. Taking turns (alternating steps): one player moves, then the other answers the new position; on the plain game the players stay at a bounded distance, circling. Not a change of the game or of its field: a change of how the field is stepped. | f = x y from (1, 1) at rate 0.2: both at once, each step multiplies the distance from the solution by 1.0198; the look-ahead step by 0.9806. | dynamics-explorer |
| neural-ode | a network as a flow | A network whose layers are the steps of an integrator following a learned vector field: the same field applied again and again moves the representation from input to output. Then a question about the network is a question about the flow: where it carries points, what it cannot do (a flow run backward cannot tear a piece in two). Not a network with many different layers; one field, stepped. | A small learned field carrying one set of points on the sphere to another, in five steps held to the sphere. | manifold-flows |

## Steps and metrics in training

| id | term | explanation | example | first demo |
| -- | ---- | ----------- | ------- | ---------- |
| parameter-space | parameter space | The room of all the numbers a model can have: every setting of its weights is one point, and training moves that point. The loss is a function on this room, and the step of training is a step through it. Not the data's room and not the representation's: three different rooms, with three different geometries. | A layer of 4 by 4 weights lives in a parameter space of 16 dimensions. | optimization-explorer |
| riemannian-gradient | Riemannian gradient | The direction of steepest rise of a function on a manifold, measured by the manifold's own metric and lying in the tangent space: the ordinary gradient with its off-surface part dropped and its length corrected by the metric. The step then goes along it with the exponential map or a retraction, and stays on the manifold. Not the plain gradient followed by putting the point back, which is a retraction of a different vector. | On the sphere, the gradient of a loss at p has its part along p removed; the step along it is cos t p + sin t v / t. | optimization-explorer |
| natural-gradient | natural gradient | A gradient step measured by the Fisher metric (Amari): instead of asking which change of the weights lowers the loss most per unit of weight change, ask which lowers it most per unit of change in what the model predicts. The step is the gradient multiplied by the inverse of the Fisher matrix. It can take fewer steps and costs more per step; whether it pays is measured, not assumed. Not a different loss: the same loss, a different ruler. | A softmax model of six weights with one input twenty times too large: plain gradient descent does not settle in 60 steps, the natural-gradient step (the exact Fisher, 6 by 6) stays under the target from step 2. | optimization-explorer |
| preconditioner | preconditioner (a step under a metric) | A matrix that reshapes a gradient step: the step is the gradient multiplied by it. The identity is plain gradient descent; a diagonal preconditioner scales each weight's step on its own (Adam's averages do this); the inverse Fisher matrix gives the natural gradient. A preconditioner is a choice of metric on the parameter space. Not a change of the loss or of the data. | On the loss (x^2 + 3 y^2) / 2 the plain step zigzags down the narrow valley; the step under the metric diag(1, 3), the loss's own curvature (the preconditioner diag(1, 1/3)), goes straight to the floor. | optimization-explorer |
| contour | contour (a level line) | A curve along which a function has one value: the height lines of a map, drawn for a loss over two numbers. Walking along a contour the loss does not change; the gradient points straight across the contours, the way the loss rises fastest. Nested contours close round a floor. Not the path of descent: a descent crosses contours, it does not follow them. | For L = (x^2 + 3 y^2) / 2 the contour L = 2 is the ellipse x^2 + 3 y^2 = 4, from x = 2 across to y = 1.15 up. | optimization-explorer |
| condition-number | condition number | For a bowl-shaped loss, the ratio of its steepest curvature to its gentlest: 1 for a round bowl, large for a long narrow valley. It bounds how fast gradient descent with the plain ruler can go: each step of an exact line search shrinks the loss by at least a factor set by it, and the larger it is the slower that is. A metric changes it: under the ruler G it is the condition number of the bowl measured by G. Not a property of the loss alone: of the loss and the ruler together. | The bowl (x^2 + 3 y^2) / 2 has condition number 3 under the plain ruler; under diag(a, 1) it is the larger of 3 a and 1 / (3 a), which is 1 at a = 1/3. | optimization-explorer |
| line-search | line search (a step size policy) | Having chosen a direction, how far to go along it. A fixed step size goes the same distance (times the gradient's size) every time; a line search looks along the line for the point where the loss is least and stops there (exact), or for a point that is low enough (inexact). With an exact line search on a bowl each step ends where the new gradient is at right angles to the old direction, so the plain steps zigzag. Not the direction: the direction is the metric's choice, the distance along it the policy's. | On (x^2 + 3 y^2) / 2 along the direction d the exact step is eta = (g . d) / (d' H d), with H = diag(1, 3). | optimization-explorer |
| eigenvector | eigenvector and eigenvalue | A direction a matrix only stretches: A v = lambda v, the number lambda the eigenvalue (stretched by lambda; shrunk if it is between -1 and 1; flipped if negative). A matrix that turns every direction, like a rotation, has no such real direction: its eigenvalues come as a complex pair, whose real part says whether repeated application shrinks or grows and whose other part how fast it turns. A symmetric n by n matrix always has n real ones at right angles; the leading one, with the largest eigenvalue, is the unit vector that makes p' A p largest (the Rayleigh quotient), which a step that stays on the sphere can climb to. Not a point: a direction, so v and -v are the same eigenvector. | For the symmetric A = (4 1 0 / 1 3 1 / 0 1 2) the eigenvalues are about 4.73, 3 and 1.27 and the leading eigenvector about (0.79, 0.58, 0.21). The rotation (0 -1 / 1 0) has the complex pair +- i: real part 0, so its paths neither shrink nor grow. | optimization-explorer |
| fisher-matrix | Fisher matrix (the Fisher metric) | A matrix with a row and a column for every weight of a model that says how much a small change of the weights changes what the model predicts: for each pair of weights, the average, over the examples and over the answers weighted by their predicted probability, of the product of the log-probability's slopes along the two weights. Used as a metric on the weights, a change that barely moves the predictions is short and one that moves them a lot is long, however the numbers are written. Not the loss's curvature in general, though for softmax regression the two are equal. It has as many entries as the square of the number of weights. | Six weights give a 6 by 6 Fisher matrix; a million give a trillion entries, which is why practical natural-gradient methods approximate it. | optimization-explorer |
| damping | damping (of a metric) | A small multiple of the identity added to a metric before it is inverted, so that a direction the metric calls almost free (an eigenvalue near 0) does not get an enormous step. It mixes in a little of the flat ruler: the step becomes partly a plain gradient step, and the metric's indifference to how the weights are written is given up in proportion. Not a change of the loss. | The natural step solves (F + 0.001 I) d = g instead of F d = g. | optimization-explorer |

## Constrained transformations

| id | term | explanation | example | first demo |
| -- | ---- | ----------- | ------- | ---------- |
| constraint-set | constraint set | The set of transformations a model is allowed to use, chosen before training: all matrices with rows and columns summing to one, all orthogonal matrices, all matrices of rank at most r. Training is kept inside it by a projection or a parameterization. Some constraint sets are smooth manifolds (the orthogonal group), some are polytopes (the doubly stochastic matrices), some are stratified sets (rank at most r); the pages say which. Not the data manifold and not the parameter space: a subset of the parameter space, with its own shape. | mHC keeps its mixing matrices doubly stochastic. | mhc-explorer |
| doubly-stochastic | doubly stochastic matrix | A square matrix with no negative entries whose every row and every column sums to one. Multiplying a set of streams by one takes a weighted average of them, with the weights summing to one along both ways, so nothing is amplified: the size of the signal cannot grow (its largest singular value is at most one) and the mean of the streams is kept exactly. The set of such matrices is the Birkhoff polytope. Not a stochastic matrix (rows only) and not an orthogonal matrix (which may have negative entries and keeps lengths, not means). | [[0.5, 0.5], [0.5, 0.5]] averages two streams; the identity leaves them alone; both are doubly stochastic. | mhc-explorer |
| sinkhorn | Sinkhorn's iteration | A way to make a matrix of positive entries doubly stochastic: divide each row by its sum, then each column by its sum, and repeat; the row and column sums both tend to one (Sinkhorn 1964; Sinkhorn and Knopp 1967). Stopped early, the result is nearly doubly stochastic, and the pages measure how nearly (the row and column sum errors). It is also, in optimal transport, the fast way to a regularized transport plan (Cuturi 2013). Not an exact projection unless run to convergence; mHC runs twenty sweeps. | From the exponential of each of a 3 by 3 table of numbers: after every sweep the columns sum to 1 exactly; the row sums' largest error falls from 5.2 to under 0.01 in three sweeps and under 1e-6 in nine. | mhc-explorer |
| singular-value | singular value | How much a matrix stretches the directions it stretches most and least: a matrix turns the ball of vectors of length 1 into an ellipsoid, and its singular values are the lengths of that ellipsoid's axes, largest first. The largest says how much the matrix can enlarge a vector in one step; repeated, a matrix whose largest singular value is under 1 shrinks every signal, but one above 1 need not grow it: the rate at which powers grow or die is the size of the largest eigenvalue, which the largest singular value only bounds from above. Not an eigenvalue in general, though for a symmetric matrix with no negative eigenvalues the two are the same. | The 3 by 3 matrix halfway from the identity to uniform mixing has singular values 1, 0.5, 0.5: it keeps the streams' mean and halves their differences. | mhc-explorer |
| mixing-gain | gain (of a mixing matrix) | How much a mixing matrix can enlarge the streams, as the mHC paper measures it: forward, the largest sum of the sizes of a row's entries (how much one output stream can draw from all the inputs); backward, the same for a column (how much a gradient can be amplified on its way back). A doubly stochastic matrix's gain is 1 both ways; a product of matrices has a gain at most the product of their gains, which is why gains are watched through depth. Not the singular value, though both bound how a signal can grow. | A free positive 3 by 3 matrix with gains 6.2 and 6.9: mixed a hundred times, a signal of length 3.2 grows past 10^71. | mhc-explorer |
| orthogonal-group | orthogonal group | The set of square matrices that keep every length and every angle: rotations and reflections. It is a smooth manifold (of dimension n(n - 1)/2 for n by n matrices), not a polytope; a step on it is kept on it by a retraction (the nearest orthogonal matrix, from the SVD). Mixing streams by an orthogonal matrix keeps the size of the signal exactly, at the price of negative entries: not an average. Not the doubly stochastic matrices, which keep means and not lengths. | A rotation of the plane by 30 degrees; the identity; a swap of two streams (a permutation, which is both orthogonal and doubly stochastic). | mhc-explorer |
| cayley-transform | Cayley transform | A way to make an orthogonal matrix from any square matrix of numbers: take S, the skew-symmetric part of the numbers (S' = -S), and form (I - S)^-1 (I + S). It is orthogonal for every S, is the identity at S = 0, and reaches every orthogonal matrix that has no eigenvalue -1; a network can learn S freely and its matrix stays orthogonal. Not the polar factor (the nearest orthogonal matrix to a given one), another way to an orthogonal matrix. | With S having 0.5 above the diagonal and -0.5 below it (2 by 2), (I - S)^-1 (I + S) is the rotation with rows (0.6, 0.8) and (-0.8, 0.6). | mhc-explorer |
| stiefel-grassmann | Stiefel and Grassmann manifolds | Two manifolds of frames and subspaces: the Stiefel manifold is the set of matrices with r orthonormal columns in n dimensions (a frame); the Grassmann manifold is the set of r-dimensional subspaces of an n-dimensional space (a frame with its basis forgotten: all frames spanning the same subspace are one point). The home of low-rank adapters' subspaces and shared bases. Not the set of matrices of rank at most r, which is not a manifold (a stratified set). | A LoRA adapter's two thin matrices span a subspace of rank r: a point of a Grassmann manifold; changing the basis of the subspace moves the frame on the Stiefel manifold and not the point on the Grassmann manifold. | geometric-weight-updates |
| stratified-set | stratified set (matrices of rank at most r) | A set made of smooth pieces of different dimensions fitted together, like a cone with its tip: smooth away from the joins, not at them. The matrices of rank at most r are one: the matrices of rank exactly r form a smooth manifold, and the matrices of smaller rank sit inside as its edge, where it is not locally flat. A step that lowers the rank crosses a join. Not a smooth manifold, and not a polytope (it has no flat faces). | The 2 by 2 matrices of rank at most 1: a cone in four dimensions whose tip is the zero matrix. | geometric-weight-updates |

## Residual streams and mHC

| id | term | explanation | example | first demo |
| -- | ---- | ----------- | ------- | ---------- |
| residual-stream | residual stream | The running vector a deep network passes from layer to layer, to which each layer adds its small change (the residual) instead of replacing it: x then x + f(x) then x + f(x) + g(x + f(x)). The stream is what makes very deep networks trainable: a layer that learns nothing passes the stream through unchanged. Not the layer's output alone; the layer's output added to what came in. | x + F(x) with F(x) = tanh(W x) on a stream of 4 numbers (part 1); a stack of layers, the size of the stream after each and of the gradient backward (part 2). | mhc-explorer |
| hyper-connections | hyper-connections | Several residual streams side by side instead of one (n copies of the hidden state), with a learned matrix mixing them at every layer and learned weights saying which streams a layer reads and writes (Zhu et al., ICLR 2025). Unconstrained, the mixing matrices multiplied through many layers can grow or shrink the signal by a large factor: the mHC paper measured a gain of about three thousand in a model of 27 billion weights (its figure, not reproduced here). Not a wider layer: the same layer, several streams. | Three streams of 4 numbers, a 3 by 3 mixing matrix (part 1); the gain of a product of many of them through a stack (part 2). | mhc-explorer |
| mhc | mHC (manifold-constrained hyper-connections) | Hyper-connections with the mixing matrices kept doubly stochastic (DeepSeek, Dec 2025, ICML 2026): the mixing is then a weighted average of the streams, which cannot enlarge them however often it is repeated, and their mean is passed through exactly; the layers' own added outputs can still grow the signal. The constraint is enforced by twenty Sinkhorn sweeps on the exponentials of learned numbers, so it is approximate (the paper measured a composite gain of about 1.6, not 1). The name says "manifold"; the set is the Birkhoff polytope, which these pages call a polytope. | A 3 by 3 matrix from Sinkhorn's iteration mixing a signal 100 times keeps its mean exactly (part 1); the gain through a stack (part 2). | mhc-explorer |
| signal-propagation | signal propagation (through depth) | How the size of the numbers changes as they pass up through a deep network's layers, and the size of the gradient, the training signal, as it passes back down: if each layer multiplies them by a factor above 1 they grow with depth (exploding), below 1 they shrink (vanishing), and either can stop a network from training. Measured layer by layer at the start of training. Not the same as how well the network learns, which needs training to see. | A stack whose layers each multiply sizes by 1.2 grows them 1.2^32, about 340 times, over 32 layers. | mhc-explorer |

## Representations

| id | term | explanation | example | first demo |
| -- | ---- | ----------- | ------- | ---------- |
| representation | representation | The way a model keeps a point: the list of numbers that stands for it, and the space those numbers are read in. The same point can be kept as it is (a flat space, measured by the straight line) or as its direction only (a point of the unit sphere, measured by the angle). Which is chosen is a choice of geometry, made before anything is learned; not the data itself, which is the same under both. | A direction of length 2 pointing up and right, kept flat, is (1.41, 1.41); kept on the sphere it is (0.71, 0.71), and its length is gone. | representation-explorer |
| great-circle | great-circle distance | The shortest way along the skin of a sphere between two of its points: along the circle cut by the plane through them and the middle, the way an airplane flies. For the unit sphere it is the angle between the two points, in radians, from 0 to pi; the straight line through the inside (the chord) is always shorter, 2 sin of half the angle. Not the cosine distance, though for unit points the two order neighbors the same way. | Two points a quarter turn apart: 1.571 along the sphere, 1.414 straight through. | sphere-hello |
| cosine-distance | cosine distance | One less the cosine of the angle between two points, where the cosine is their product summed over the coordinates divided by their two lengths: 0 for points that point the same way, 1 for points at right angles, 2 for opposite ones. It ignores the lengths, as the angle does, and orders pairs as the angle does, since the cosine only falls as the angle grows; it is not the angle, and is not a distance along any space. | Two points at a quarter turn: cosine 0, cosine distance 1; at a tenth of a radian: cosine distance 0.005. | representation-explorer |
| nearest-neighbors | nearest neighbors (k-NN) | The plainest way to classify: keep the training points with their labels, and for a new point find the k training points nearest it, under whichever distance was chosen, and answer with the label most of them have. It learns no number, so the distance is all the geometry it has; change the distance and the neighbors change. Not a trained model, though it is a classifier. | With k = 5 and the straight line, a point whose five nearest are three of class 1 and two of class 2 is called class 1. | baseline-knn |
| decision-boundary | decision boundary | The line (or surface) where a classifier changes its answer: on one side it says one class, on the other another. Drawn by asking the classifier at every point of a grid and coloring each by its answer. Under the straight line the boundary between two clusters is a line midway between them; under the angle it is a ray from the middle, since every point of a ray is the same direction. | On the directions, the straight line's boundary moves when the point's length changes and the angle's does not; the page draws it at three lengths. | representation-explorer |
| held-out | held out | Points set aside before training, never shown to the model, on which it is tested: the share of them it labels right is the held-out accuracy, the measure of what was learned rather than remembered. Here a seeded quarter of every set, the same quarter for every geometry compared. Not the training points, whose accuracy says only what was fitted. | 120 points: 90 trained on, 30 held out; 27 of the 30 right is 0.9. | baseline-knn |
| cross-entropy | cross-entropy loss | The number a classifier is trained to lower: for each training point, minus the logarithm of the probability the model gives its true class, averaged over the points. 0 when every true class gets probability 1; log of the number of classes (1.10 for three, 1.39 for four) when the model guesses evenly. It falls as the model grows sure of the right answers; the held-out accuracy is measured apart. | A model giving the true class probability 0.5 on every point has loss 0.69. | representation-explorer |
| overfitting | overfitting | What a model does when it keeps lowering its training loss by remembering its training points rather than by learning their structure: the training loss falls while the held-out accuracy stops rising or falls. More steps, or more numbers, make it likelier on a small set. Not a property of a geometry; a sign that a comparison of geometries must hold the steps and the numbers equal and look at the held-out figure. | A loss that falls from 0.5 to 0.3 while the held-out accuracy goes from 0.75 to 0.675. | representation-explorer |
| poincare-disk | the Poincare disk (hyperbolic space) | A space of negative curvature drawn inside a circle: the points are those inside the unit circle, and a small step near the rim counts for far more than the same step near the middle (by 2 / (1 - r^2) at plain radius r), so the rim is infinitely far away and never reached. Room grows fast with distance from any point: a circle of radius L has length 2 pi sinh L, which doubles about every 0.7 of radius, as the levels of a tree double. In three or more dimensions the same thing is the Poincare ball. Not the unit disk of the plane, though drawn in it: the picture's lengths are not the space's. | The point at plain radius 0.5 is 1.10 from the middle; at 0.9, 2.94; at 0.99999, the rim's radius the points are kept to, 12.2. | representation-explorer |
| embedding | embedding (a fit of distances) | Putting a set of things into a space as points so that the distances between the points come as close as can be to given distances between the things: here the nodes of a tree into the plane, the sphere or the disk so that the path lengths along the tree are kept. Found by gradient descent on the distortion: each node moved a little along the space against the gradient, many times. Not a representation chosen by a rule (the directions of part 1 were a rule): a fit, with a start, a step count and a rate, which can end in a local minimum. | Thirty-one nodes fitted in the disk for 200 steps from a seeded start, their distances 0.10 off the tree's on average. | representation-explorer |
| tree-distance | tree distance (a hierarchy's own distance) | The number of edges on the one path between two nodes of a tree: parent to child 1, siblings 2, two leaves on different branches of the root 2 times the depth. A tree's distances are what a hierarchy (a taxonomy, a file system) says about how far apart its things are, and no flat picture keeps them: leaves of neighboring branches that look near are far along the tree. Not the straight-line distance of a drawing of the tree. | In the tree of depth 4 and branching 2, nodes 16 and 31 (the first and the last leaf) are 8 apart. | baseline-knn |
| local-minimum | local minimum | A place a fit by gradient descent can stop that is not the best: every small move makes the loss worse, so the steps stop, though a better arrangement exists farther away. Different starts can end in different ones, which is why a fit is run from several seeded starts and the spread between them is reported. Not a property of the space being fitted into alone: the start and the rate matter. | The plane's fit of the tree ends at a distortion of 0.15 from two starts and at 0.30 from a third. | representation-explorer |
| logistic-classifier | logistic classifier | The simplest trained classifier: each class gets a score that is a weighted sum of the point's coordinates plus a bias, softmax turns the scores into probabilities, and the weights are set by gradient descent on the cross-entropy; its decision boundary is straight (a line, a plane) in the coordinates it is given. Also called softmax regression. Not a network with a hidden layer, which can draw bent boundaries. | On the tree's embedded coordinates, 200 steps at rate 0.5: 6 of the 7 held-out nodes right in the plane and in the disk. | representation-explorer |
