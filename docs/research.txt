Manifolds, vector fields, and the geometry of machine learning
Your question connects several important areas of ML research: manifold learning, geometric deep learning, dynamical systems, optimization, and learned neural-network architectures.

The particularly interesting possibility is that instead of merely training weights within a fixed neural-network architecture, we could change the geometry of the space in which learning and inference occur.

That suggests several experiments for X_eTaL, including ones that could complement your work on mHC, LoRA, shared subspaces, and alternatives to backpropagation.

2. What exactly is a manifold?
A manifold is a space that looks like ordinary Euclidean space when examined locally, even if its overall geometry is curved or topologically complicated.

A sphere's surface, for example, is two-dimensional even though we normally represent it using three coordinates.

The distinction is between:

Ambient dimension: the number of coordinates used to represent the object.

Intrinsic dimension: the number of independent coordinates needed to move around within it.

A neural network might use 4,096-dimensional embeddings, while the representations relevant to a particular task occupy a substantially lower-dimensional structure.

This is the manifold hypothesis. It is a useful modeling assumption, not a guarantee that every learned representation lies on a single smooth manifold.

10. The deeper question: can we change topology during learning?
This is where the problem becomes particularly interesting.

A continuous, invertible flow on a fixed manifold preserves its topology. It can stretch, bend, and redistribute points, but it cannot tear a connected manifold into two disconnected pieces.

To change topology, we need something beyond an ordinary diffeomorphic flow, such as a noninvertible transformation, a change in the underlying space, or an embedding into a larger space followed by a suitable projection.

That leads to a useful experimental distinction:

Geometry learning: Change distances, curvature, or metric.

Manifold learning: Discover or modify the representation space.

Topology-changing learning: Allow the structure of the space itself to change.

Architecture learning: Change the computational graph or allowed transformations.

These are related, but they are not equivalent.

For an initial experiment, train a small network on two interlocking circles or a Swiss-roll dataset. At each layer, visualize the representation and its neighborhood graph. Then compare a fixed Euclidean embedding, a learned-curvature embedding, and an augmented-dimensional flow.

This makes the differences between bending, separating, and changing topology much easier to see.

My recommended starting point
I would build the general-purpose Manifold and Vector Field Explorer first, with three linked views: the manifold itself, the tangent vector field on it, and the optimization or inference trajectory over time.

Then add a fourth view showing how a neural network transforms the representation from layer to layer.

This creates an educational foundation that can subsequently support mHC, geometric LoRA, learned metrics, neural ODEs, and geometry-aware weight-space search—without coupling the general mathematics to one particular fine-tuning method.

The most interesting long-term research question is not simply which manifold is best? It is:

Can an ML system learn which geometry, metric, dimension, and constraints are appropriate for each layer, task, or stage of training—and can we demonstrate that this improves learning efficiency or generalization?

That is a much broader architectural direction than mHC alone, and it is worth exploring systematically.

6. Establish a standard educational format for every demo
One lesson from your LoRA Explorer work is that an interactive visualization alone does not necessarily teach the underlying algorithm.

I would make every demo follow the same progression:

Intuition

Explain the ML problem in plain language, with a simple geometric illustration.

Mathematics

Show the equations, explain every variable, and connect the mathematical operations to the visualization.

Interactive experiment

Change geometry, curvature, dimension, metrics, or constraints and observe the consequences.

Actual ML training or inference

Run a small reproducible model, not just a mathematical animation.

Analysis and comparison

Show accuracy, convergence, distortion, stability, and computational costs.

Research extension

Explain what the experiment establishes, what remains unknown, and which papers motivate further investigation.

A useful feature would be a synchronized equation, tensor, and geometry view. Selecting an operation in an X_eTaL tensor pipeline could highlight the corresponding vectors, tangent spaces, or matrix transformations.

That would distinguish this project from most manifold visualization tools.

7. Focus the research on three testable hypotheses
Rather than accumulating unrelated papers, I would establish three overarching hypotheses.

H1 — Geometry matching
A representation space whose geometry matches the structure of the task can improve learning efficiency or generalization.

Test: Compare Euclidean, spherical, and hyperbolic embeddings on flat, directional, and hierarchical datasets.

H2 — Geometric constraints
Constraining neural-network transformations can improve numerical stability and information propagation.

Test: Compare unconstrained, doubly stochastic, and orthogonal mixing in small residual networks.

H3 — Adaptive geometry
Learning geometric properties jointly with model parameters can outperform fixed geometry on heterogeneous tasks.

Test: Compare fixed curvature, trainable curvature, and mixed-curvature representations on the same datasets.

For H3, recent work on adaptive manifold curvature regularization and mixed-curvature geometric learning provides useful starting points.

The distinctive opportunity is to make this more than a visualization gallery: a small, reproducible laboratory where we can change the geometry of an ML algorithm and observe exactly what improves, degrades, or remains unchanged.
